01 — what a censor sees

Two kinds of observer

A censor can watch a connection, or it can poke at the server. Samizdat is built so that both see a browser and a website. The client connects to the server's IP address and names a cover site in the TLS handshake. The server is not that site. It holds the key to tell its own clients from everyone else, and hands everyone else to the real cover site.

Watching the wire

  1. A TLS 1.3 ClientHello from uTLS with Chrome's fingerprint, naming the cover site.
  2. The authentication tag sits in the 32-byte SessionID, which browsers fill with random bytes in every TLS 1.3 ClientHello anyway. It's sealed to the server's key and to that exact ClientHello, and no part of it repeats between connections.
  3. The first ten TLS records after the handshake follow sizes and directions drawn from a model of real TLS flows, not the fixed opening of an HTTP/2 tunnel.
  4. After that, a long-lived HTTP/2 connection carries the tunnelled streams, up to 100 at a time, like a browser's.

Probing the server

  1. Anything without a valid tag is forwarded byte for byte to the real cover site the server is configured with: a ClientHello that fails, one that has already been used, or bytes that aren't TLS at all.
  2. The prober completes a real handshake with the cover site's real certificate and gets its real HTTP responses. The server doesn't rewrite or decrypt any of it.
  3. Replaying a captured ClientHello doesn't work either: the server remembers the SessionIDs it has accepted for 24 to 48 hours (less under very heavy load), and a version 2 tag expires 12 hours either side of the server's clock.
  4. Clients can pin the server's certificate, so a client that's been sent to the cover site fails instead of talking to it.
02 — the first packets give it away

What CenKL found

In late 2025, researchers at the University of Michigan and elsewhere recorded 326,531,763 real TLS flows at a regional ISP and built a model of how real TLS connections begin: the size and direction of each packet after the handshake. They then measured how far nine circumvention protocols stray from it, using KL divergence (in nats; 0 means identical).

Their conclusion: all of them are distinguishable from real-world TLS, as early as the first post-handshake packet.

1208 bytes

Trojan, VMess, VLESS and XTLS Vision send unusually many 1,208-byte packets in their first three packets after the handshake. Real TLS does that at the first packet only 0.38% of the time. The cause is Go's dynamic TLS record sizing, which Xray leaves on.

The authors' countermeasure, ProxyShaper, reshapes a connection's first 10 TLS records with sizes and directions drawn from their model. Against VLESS, it cut a gradient-boosted classifier's precision from 0.977 to 0.830. It helps; it doesn't make a proxy invisible.

Samizdat, measured against the same model

Samizdat now does what ProxyShaper does, by default at both ends. Both sides derive a shared seed from the TLS session's exported keying material, draw the same ten-record schedule from CenKL's released order-1 Markov model, and send exactly those record sizes in those directions, carrying HTTP/2 data inside and padding the rest. It also turns off Go's dynamic record sizing and drops TLS session tickets, which added a tell of their own.

Nats of KL divergence from CenKL's size-and-direction model, first 10 packets after the handshake, 800 flows each on loopback, segmented at a 1,448-byte MSS. The floor isn't zero because 800 flows is a finite sample: real TLS drawn from the model itself scores 11.3. These are our measurements with the authors' released model, not the paper's; see samizdat#29.

The 1,448-byte cap matters. CenKL's model counts 1,460-byte packets, from senders without TCP timestamps. Linux servers send timestamps and fit 1,448 bytes, so a scheduled 1,460-byte record leaves as two packets, 1,448 and 12. The paper notes the same mismatch in its own testbed. Samizdat leaves sizes above 1,448 out of the schedule.

REALITY is the closest relative

Samizdat's version 2 authentication is REALITY's idea, implemented independently: an X25519 secret between the client's ordinary key share and the server's static key, an AES-GCM seal over a time and a short ID, and the whole ClientHello as associated data, so a copied tag fails in any other handshake. The differences are in what surrounds it: samizdat remembers what it has accepted, checks the time by default, doesn't need the cover site to be reachable for its own clients, multiplexes over HTTP/2, and shapes the start of the connection. REALITY hands authenticated clients a throwaway certificate signed with the shared key; samizdat serves its own and lets clients pin it.

04 — under the hood

How it works

AAuthentication in the SessionID

The client takes an X25519 secret between its own key share and the server's public key, derives an AES-256-GCM key with HKDF over the ClientHello's random, and seals version ‖ time ‖ short ID with the rest of the ClientHello as associated data. The 32 bytes that come out replace the SessionID. Older clients send version 1, an HMAC tag; servers accept both until told otherwise.

BMasquerade

A connection that doesn't authenticate is spliced, raw, to the cover site's real address. The server reads only the first record it needs to check the tag, sends it on to the cover site, and copies bytes both ways, without rewriting them or terminating TLS, until both sides close or ten minutes pass. That includes connections that never send a ClientHello: whatever arrived goes to the cover site, which decides how to answer.

CConnection-start shaping

Both ends export keying material from the finished TLS session. 32 bytes seed a ChaCha8 sampler that draws ten records' sizes and directions from the model; 8 bytes key an HMAC over the schedule, and its first 8 bytes are a marker that tells the server this client shapes. During the schedule each side sends exactly its scheduled records, carrying queued HTTP/2 bytes and padding. After the tenth, data passes straight through.

DOne connection, many streams

Every proxied connection is an HTTP/2 CONNECT stream on a long-lived TLS connection that carries up to 100 of them, so a censor counting TLS connections to an address sees few. Servers run samizdat's own lean HTTP/2 server, which batches frames into fewer TLS records and system calls: 66–90% less server CPU per gigabyte downloaded than Go's standard one in benchmarks, with the same frames on the wire.

EFragmented ClientHello

Optionally, the ClientHello is written in pieces split around the SNI, with a short random delay, in the style of Geneva, so a middlebox that reads the SNI from a single packet often doesn't find it there. It's best effort: the split can fall just outside the hostname.

FLegacy clients keep working

A server reads the first 8 bytes after the handshake. The shaping marker means a shaping client; an HTTP/2 preface means an older one, which is served exactly as before. New servers serve every client released so far; new clients need new servers.

05 — what it doesn't do

Limits

No proxy that carries real traffic is invisible. These are the gaps we know about.

  • Timing isn't shaped. CenKL's timing-aware classifier still reaches about 0.9 recall against shaped traffic. Samizdat used to add a random 1–30 ms delay before each write; it was removed because Xue et al. (NDSS 2025) found that random delays like obfs4's can make cross-layer RTT fingerprinting easier, not harder.
  • Tunnelled TLS is still inside. Applications using samizdat bring their own TLS, so there is TLS inside samizdat's TLS. Multiplexing helps most when several streams share the connection; Xue et al. (USENIX Security 2024) found it far weaker with only one.
  • Shaping covers ten records. After that, traffic is whatever the tunnelled applications send. The schedule fixes TLS record sizes; TCP can still split or merge them into packets.
  • The model is one network's view. It was built from one regional ISP in late 2025. Real TLS elsewhere, or later, may look different, and a censor with its own data can build its own model.
  • The replay record lives in memory. A server restart forgets the SessionIDs it has accepted, and more than a million in one window shortens how long they're kept. Version 2's 12-hour time limit still applies.
  • Not independently reviewed. The version 2 authentication hasn't had an outside cryptographic review, and the measurements on this page are ours, on loopback.
06 — use it

Use it

The easiest way to use Samizdat is Lantern, the free app for Android, iOS, Windows, macOS and Linux, which uses it among its protocols. For developers, Samizdat is a Go library with no sing-box dependency, and an inbound and outbound in lantern-box.

go get github.com/getlantern/samizdat

client, err := samizdat.NewClient(samizdat.ClientConfig{
    ServerAddr:  "203.0.113.7:443",
    ServerName:  "cover.example",   // the cover site named in the handshake
    PublicKey:   serverPublicKey,   // 32-byte X25519 public key
    ShortID:     shortID,           // 8-byte client identifier
    Fingerprint: "chrome",
})
conn, err := client.DialContext(ctx, "tcp", "example.com:443")
// lantern-box (sing-box) outbound
{
  "type": "samizdat",
  "tag": "samizdat-out",
  "server": "203.0.113.7",
  "server_port": 443,
  "public_key": "<64 hex chars>",
  "short_id": "<16 hex chars>",
  "server_name": "cover.example",
  "cert_spki_sha256": "<optional pin, 64 hex chars>"
}

Generate keys with go run ./cmd/samizdat-server --genkeys. Pick a cover site that's reachable from the server, serves TLS 1.3 with HTTP/2, and is unremarkable on the network you're getting around.

07 — questions

FAQ

Q. What is Samizdat?

A. Samizdat is an open-source censorship-circumvention protocol from Lantern. It carries proxy traffic inside TLS 1.3 and HTTP/2 connections that look like a browser visiting a real website. The server authenticates its own clients inside the TLS handshake and hands everything else to a real cover site, and the first records after the handshake are shaped to match a model of real TLS traffic.

Q. How is Samizdat different from VLESS and XTLS Vision?

A. VLESS sends its client ID inside TLS and falls back to a server the operator runs, behind the operator's own certificate. Vision adds padding to the inner TLS handshake. Neither shapes the start of the connection: CenKL (IEEE S&P 2027) found both send telltale 1,208-byte packets right after the handshake, and 71.7% of Vision connections start with one. Samizdat sends probes to a real cover site, multiplexes over HTTP/2, turns off the record sizing behind those 1,208-byte packets, and shapes its first 10 records. See the comparison.

Q. Is Samizdat the same as REALITY?

A. No, but its version 2 authentication follows REALITY's design: an X25519 secret between the client's key share and the server's key, sealing a time and short ID into the TLS SessionID, bound to the whole ClientHello. Samizdat adds a replay filter (REALITY has none, and a 2026 proof of concept detects REALITY by replaying handshakes), checks the time by default, doesn't need the cover site to be reachable for its own clients, multiplexes over HTTP/2, and shapes the start of each connection.

Q. Can censors detect Samizdat?

A. No proxy that carries real traffic is invisible, and nobody outside Lantern has evaluated Samizdat yet. Measured against CenKL's model of 326.5 million real TLS flows, Samizdat's connection starts score 12.4 nats of divergence, against 11.3 for real TLS sampled from the same model and 63.0 before shaping. Timing isn't shaped, and tunnelled TLS is still inside the connection; see Limits.

Q. How does Samizdat resist active probing?

A. A handshake without a valid authentication tag, or one that has been used before, is forwarded byte for byte to the real cover site, so a prober sees that site's certificate, TLS behaviour and pages. The server remembers the SessionIDs it has accepted for 24 to 48 hours, and version 2 tags expire 12 hours either side of the server's clock, so replaying a captured handshake doesn't work either.

Q. What is connection-start shaping?

A. Researchers found that proxies stand out in the first few packets after the TLS handshake, before much user data flows. Samizdat's client and server derive a shared seed from the TLS session, draw the same schedule of 10 record sizes and directions from CenKL's model of real TLS, and send exactly those records, carrying their own data and padding the rest. After the tenth record, data passes straight through.

Q. Does Samizdat avoid TLS-in-TLS detection?

A. Partly. Applications tunnelled through Samizdat bring their own TLS, so there is TLS inside Samizdat's TLS, as with any proxy. Samizdat multiplexes every stream over HTTP/2, which Xue et al. (USENIX Security 2024) found lowers their attack's true positive rate by over 70% when two or more streams share a connection, and much less with one. Samizdat hasn't been measured against that attack.

Q. How is Samizdat different from Shadowsocks?

A. Shadowsocks traffic is encrypted from its first byte, and China's Great Firewall has flagged it by the length and entropy of the first packet (IMC 2020). Samizdat opens with a standard TLS 1.3 ClientHello with Chrome's fingerprint, like a browser.

Q. Does Samizdat use UDP or QUIC?

A. No. Samizdat runs over TCP only, so networks that block UDP, as some in Russia do, don't affect it.

Q. Is Samizdat open source?

A. Yes. Samizdat is written in Go and released under the Apache License 2.0 at github.com/getlantern/samizdat. The library has no sing-box dependency; lantern-box adds a sing-box inbound and outbound.

Q. How do I use Samizdat?

A. The easiest way is to download Lantern, which uses Samizdat among its protocols. Developers can use the Go library or lantern-box's sing-box inbound and outbound; see Use it.

Q. Who makes Samizdat?

A. Lantern, a nonprofit VPN and censorship-circumvention project run by Brave New Software Project, Inc.

Q. Why is it called Samizdat?

A. Samizdat (Russian самиздат, "self-published") was the practice of copying banned writing by hand and passing it on in secret in the Soviet Union.
